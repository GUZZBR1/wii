# Detecting Running in Place and Jumping from the Torso Alone — Feasibility Without Visible Knees or Ankles

Date: November 25, 2026 · Research based on **primary sources**: running biomechanics literature (PubMed/PMC/arXiv), IMU validation literature, the BlazePose paper (arXiv 2006.10204) and BlazePose GHUM paper (arXiv 2206.11678), the official model card and documentation (ai.google.dev, ML Kit, Garmin manual), issues in `google-ai-edge/mediapipe`, and open-source webcam exergame projects. Complements `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` (jump re-arming, jitter), `docs/pesquisa-inclinacao-tronco.md` (world landmarks), `docs/autocalibracao-tolerancia-perda.md` (`visibility`/`presence` semantics), and `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md` (current running bottlenecks).

**Question:** Can running in place (cadence/rhythm) and jumping be detected using only shoulder/hip/head motion — without visible knees, ankles, or feet — and can calibration be torso-only? Today, `AutoCalibrator` requires shoulders + wrists + hips + knees with confidence ≥0.55, and cadence is measured from knee lift (`src/cv/autoCalibrator.ts`, `src/kinematics/cadenceTracker.ts`; see `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md`, “What is this state?”).

> Method note: this research was conducted using web search only (abstracts, summaries, and indexed excerpts), without reading full text beyond what those excerpts expose. Where only an abstract was available, that is indicated. No figures are invented: where a source provides no value, the gap is stated.

## 1. Vertical oscillation of the torso/head/shoulders during running — amplitude and relation to cadence

### Conclusion

Running is a ballistic (“bouncing gait”) pattern: the center of mass (CoM), pelvis, and torso oscillate vertically **once per step** — the vertical oscillation frequency is exactly the cadence in steps per second (half the stride frequency). Peak-to-peak pelvic/CoM amplitude on a treadmill is typically **6.5–13 cm** (means around 7–9 cm), and the torso oscillates **as much as or slightly more** than the CoM (systematically about +1.7 cm). The head is partially stabilized, but even there the dominant movement component falls in the step-frequency range. No direct published measurement of torso vertical amplitude during running in place was found, but all evidence suggests oscillation is at least comparable and probably greater (see items 7–8 below). **The vertical hip/shoulder signal is, in principle, the right cadence channel: it is large (centimeters), occurs at the exact step frequency, and is available without any leg landmark.**

### Cited findings

1. **Two vertical oscillations per stride = one per step in symmetric gaits.** “For a given gait, the CoM will either oscillate once or twice per stride. Symmetrical gaits, such as bipedal walking and running, have two vertical oscillations per stride. These oscillations are achieved alternately by left and right legs.” Source: [Linking Gait Dynamics to Mechanical Cost of Legged Locomotion, Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/robotics-and-ai/articles/10.3389/frobt.2018.00111/full). During running, the CoM reaches its highest point in the aerial phase and its lowest around mid-stance, when the hip, knee, and ankle are flexed — in other words, **each** contact produces one complete down-up cycle. Source: classical running mechanics summarized in a doctoral thesis citing Bramble & Lieberman 2004 and McMahon — [Giovanelli, University of Udine](https://air.uniud.it/retrieve/e27ce0c5-6088-055e-e053-6605fe0a7873/10990_676_PhD%20thesis%20final_Giovanelli_print.pdf); spring-mass model by Blickhan/McMahon-Cheng — [review in Integrative and Comparative Biology](https://academic.oup.com/icb/article/62/5/1320/6604836).

2. **Treadmill running CoM vertical oscillation: mean around 9 cm, range 6.5–13.1 cm.** Measured using markerless motion capture in 21 experienced runners, at 3 self-selected speeds over 3 sessions: means of 9.0/9.1/9.3 cm ±1.3–1.6, individual range 6.5–13.1 cm, excellent reliability (ICC 0.909). Source: [MSSE abstract, doi 10.1249/01.mss.0001061236.55291.a0](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.mss.0001061236.55291.a0). Reference values from 860 recreational treadmill runners (instrumented treadmill, GRF) are around **73–80 mm**, depending on the table stratum. Source: [Malisoux et al. 2023, PMC10588426](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10588426/) (indexed table excerpt: “Vertical oscillation, mm | 77 ± 12 | 80 ± 13 | 73 ± 11”). Garmin’s official chest-measured VO zones are: <6.4 cm (above 95th percentile), 6.4–8.1, 8.2–9.7 (median range), 9.8–11.5, and >11.5 cm; waist values are slightly larger (median 9.0–10.9 cm). Source: [Garmin fēnix/Instinct manual — Vertical Oscillation and Vertical Ratio Data](https://www8.garmin.com/manuals/webhelp/GUID-708A8F4D-9A78-49CF-9528-DE109BBCC472/EN-US/GUID-EE9E7F6F-49BE-4452-82E6-B40371D0AEC1.html).

3. **The pelvis (sacrum) reproduces CoM vertical oscillation with systematic error of 0–1.5 mm.** In 13 subjects running at 10–22 km/h, systematic sacrum × CoM discrepancy was 0–1.5 mm (decreasing with speed); within-subject random discrepancy was about 7 mm. Source: [Gullstrand et al. 2009, Gait & Posture, PubMed 19356933](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19356933/).

4. **The torso oscillates as much as — and systematically slightly more than — the CoM: overestimation by 16.3–17.6 mm.** A commercial torso-mounted accelerometer was validated against single-marker torso kinematics and GRF in 22 semi-elite runners at 2.7–3.9 m/s: agreement with the torso marker was excellent (CCC >0.97), but both the marker and accelerometer **overestimated** CoM VO by 16.27–17.56 mm — so the torso segment has a *greater*, not smaller, vertical oscillation amplitude than the CoM. Source: [Watari et al., J Applied Biomechanics 32(3):306–310, doi 10.1123/jab.2015-0200](https://doi.org/10.1123/jab.2015-0200) (abstract; secondary citations identify the device as a Garmin Forerunner 620 with chest strap). This is consistent with wearable validation that uses **a single torso marker** as the video reference for VO: mean reference value 9.4 ± 1.8 cm across 5 speeds (devices measured 8.0–10.8 cm). Source: [“The validity and reliability of wearable devices for the measurement of vertical oscillation for running,” PLOS ONE 2022 / PMC9671438](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9671438/).

5. **The head is stabilized, but not motionless — and the dominant component of head movement during running is in the step-frequency range.** Pozzo, Berthoz & Lefort measured head vertical translation under four conditions — free walking, walking in place, **running in place**, and hopping: head vertical translation amplitude ranged from **1 to 25 cm** and velocity from 0.15–1.8 m/s *across all four tasks* (the abstract does not break results down by condition); dominant translation/rotation frequencies across tasks were **0.4–3.5 Hz**, with harmonics up to 6–8 Hz. Source: [Pozzo et al. 1990, Exp Brain Res 82(1):97–106, doi 10.1007/BF00230842](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00230842) (abstract). During running, the median vertical-acceleration spectrum frequency decreases from 11–13 Hz at the shin to 7–9 Hz at the sacrum and **3–4 Hz at the head** — that is, the motion reaching the head is dominated by lower frequencies (the step range), while high-frequency impact shock is attenuated. Source: values reported in shock-acceleration literature as cited in [“Shock accelerations and attenuation in downhill and level running,” Clinical Biomechanics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0268003399000339) (indexed excerpt; figures are from that paper’s references [11] and [20], not new measurements); review in [Brain Sciences 10(3):174](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/10/3/174) (tibial impact of ~6–8 g attenuated to ~1 g at the head — this refers to *impact shock*, not sway at step frequency).

6. **Typical cadence: 150–170 spm for recreational continuous running; about 172–178 spm measured during running in place.** A systematic review states: “Typical cadence values in recreational runners range from 150 to 170 steps per minute, while elite runners often exceed 180” — [PMC12440572](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12440572/). In 25 middle-aged adults running in place, self-selected cadence was **172.35 spm**; under ultra-slow jogging at 180 bpm, it was **178.44 spm**. Source: [Hsieh, Liang & Chen 2026, BMC Sports Sci Med Rehabil, doi 10.1186/s13102-026-02126-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13102-026-02126-5). Thus the vertical signal during running in place typically falls around **2.5–3.0 Hz** (150–180 spm), with headroom to about 3.2 Hz and above.

### Running in place versus translational running — what is known and what is missing

7. **Running in place is mechanically running**: it has measurable stance and flight phases on a force plate — the running-in-place GCT validation study uses vertical-force contact/flight detection as its gold standard (11 subjects, 3 self-paced 20-second bouts at slow/medium/fast pace); wearable algorithms calibrated for translational running **fail** in place, with bias up to −81.5 ms for the Garmin Pod. Source: [Drobnič et al. 2023, Sensors 23(16):7155](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/23/16/7155). A smart patch validated in the same lab measures running-in-place **cadence** with bias <1 step/min at low/medium intensities. Source: [“A Comparison of a Novel Stretchable Smart Patch for Measuring Runner's Step Rates...,” Sensors 22(13):4897 / PMC9269156](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9269156/).

8. **The body oscillates more, not less, during slow running:** during slow jogging (1.5–3.0 m/s), vertical-force peaks and loading rates were >50% *greater* than during walking or fast running — “Impact forces were greatest when the subjects adopted a **higher, less fixed centre of gravity** during slow jogging.” Source: [Keller et al. 1996, Clin Biomech, PubMed 11415629](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11415629/) (abstract).

9. **Treadmill versus ground:** vertical oscillation on a treadmill is about 1.5 cm *lower* than during ground running (meta-analysis, low GRADE evidence; MD −1.5 cm [−2.7, −0.8]) — surface differences exist but are around 1 cm, not an order of magnitude. Source: [Van Hooren et al. 2020, Sports Medicine, doi 10.1007/s40279-019-01237-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-019-01237-z).

### Inferences (my own — not stated by the sources)

- Running in place is the zero-horizontal-speed limit of running: without horizontal propulsion, all elastic rebound energy appears as vertical movement, and item 8 indicates that the slow/ballistic regime has a *less fixed* CoM. The evidence-based expectation is that pelvic/torso vertical amplitude is **of the same order or greater** than the 6.5–13 cm in translational running — but individual style dominates: someone who “marches quickly” with barely lifted feet will have much less amplitude than someone who bounces on every step. **No published figure exists: measure it in the app.**
- For detector design, the strongest and most directly available BlazePose signal is the **image-space y-coordinate of the hip midpoint (23/24)** — close to the sacrum, which item 3 shows tracks the CoM well — and shoulder y (11/12), rigidly coupled through the torso (item 4). The head is the weakest link (partially stabilized, item 5): use it as corroboration, not the primary channel.
- In game units: image-space torso height ≈ 0.45–0.55 m, so 6–13 cm peak-to-peak ≈ **0.11–0.29 torso heights peak-to-peak**, half that on either side of the mean. Even the pessimistic floor (~0.05–0.06 torso per half-cycle) is large compared with resting jitter (see `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` §3 — must be measured) and current jump-detector thresholds (neutral band 0.03, trigger 0.08). This confirms the risk already identified in item 2 of `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md` (bounce and jump share the signal) and sizes the problem: **running oscillation is large enough to serve as a cadence signal — and large enough to confuse a naive jump detector**.

### Gaps

- **No published measurement of torso/pelvis vertical oscillation amplitude while running in place** (all VO literature is translational treadmill/ground running). This is the central gap and must be measured in the app with real pose data.
- **Head vertical amplitude specifically during running**, in centimeters: Pozzo reports the aggregate range for all four tasks (1–25 cm), but the per-condition breakdown is not in the abstract; head-stabilization literature focuses on rotation and impact shock, not displacement at step frequency.
- Amplitude as a function of cadence in the same subject (VO is known to decrease when cadence rises by +5/+10% — [Somma & Lawrence 2025, PubMed 39714230](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39714230/) — but the full relationship was not extracted).

## 2. Estimating cadence from torso signals — IMU validation literature

### Conclusion

Estimating walking/running cadence from **a single torso accelerometer** is a solved problem in wearable literature: typical absolute errors are **0.5–1.5 steps/min** with 3.5–6 s windows, using methods that also apply to a vertical hip/shoulder position signal (peak detection, autocorrelation, FFT). Torso vertical acceleration oscillates at **step frequency** (symmetric across legs). Be careful with alternative signals: axial shoulder rotation and arm swing tend to lock to **stride frequency** (half cadence) — the vertical channel is the only one directly at step frequency.

### Cited findings

1. **Cadence accuracy from a single torso accelerometer: bias −0.01 ± 0.69 steps/min (time-domain method) and 0.02 ± 1.33 steps/min (FFT), validated against instrumented insoles.** Six-second windows with 5-second overlap — one new estimate per second; run/walk classification accuracy 98%. Source: [Run4Vit, EMBC 2022, doi 10.1109/EMBC48229.2022.9871713](https://doi.org/10.1109/embc48229.2022.9871713) (abstract + indexed methods excerpt). Absolute cadence error of 0.51–0.88 steps/min (typically developing children), using peak detection on the torso acceleration norm with heuristic peak-selection rules. Source: [JNER 2019, doi 10.1186/s12984-019-0494-z](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12984-019-0494-z).

2. **Classic method: torso-acceleration autocorrelation (Moe-Nilssen & Helbostad)** — the first dominant autocorrelation period (Ad1) corresponds to step time and the second (Ad2) to stride time; Ad1 measures step-to-step regularity. In the vertical direction, Ad1 is typically positive (left and right steps produce symmetric vertical signatures), while in the mediolateral direction Ad1 is typically *negative* — direct evidence that **the torso’s vertical signal repeats every step**, whereas the lateral signal repeats each stride. Sources: [Moe-Nilssen & Helbostad 2004, J Biomechanics, doi 10.1016/s0021-9290(03)00233-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9290(03)00233-1); Ad1/Ad2 definitions in [theses/reviews applying the method](https://vtechworks.lib.vt.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/f6b60b0b-af1d-45c4-932f-6a084af65f02/content) and [MDPI Symmetry 13(9):1560](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-8994/13/9/1560). An embedded real-time implementation chose a fixed **3.5 s** window because it contains about 6 steps at 105 spm (“should be sufficient for autocorrelation processing”) — [“Real-Time Gait Cycle Parameter Recognition Using a Wearable Accelerometry System,” Sensors 11(8):7314](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/11/8/7314).

3. **A real-time cadence algorithm robust to sensor position (correlation + Sylvester criterion): mean accuracy 98.9 ± 0.2%**, true positives 85.6–100%, false positives 0–2.9%, evaluated in walking, running (3.4 m/s), and sprinting with 10 sensor positions — conventional correlation methods reached only 73–87%. Source: [medengphy 2017, doi 10.1016/j.medengphy.2017.12.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.medengphy.2017.12.007) (abstract).

4. **Arm-leg coupling:** the arms are passive “mass dampers” driven by lower-torso movement; upper-torso movement is driven by the legs, and shoulder/pelvis rotation is coordinated in anti-phase on the axial axis. Source: [Pontzer et al. 2009, J Exp Biology 212(4):523](https://journals.biologists.com/jeb/article/212/4/523/18953/Control-and-function-of-arm-swing-in-human-walking). At normal walking speeds, arm-swing frequency locks to **stride** frequency, not step frequency — [“Resonant frequencies of arms and legs identify different walking patterns,” PubMed 10831760](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10831760/) (1:1 lock with stride occurs from 0.8 m/s; only at very low speeds do the arms synchronize with each step). Shoulder acceleration contains both step and stride frequency components, with stride-frequency components in anti-phase between the shoulders — [“Biomechanical mechanism for transitions in phase and frequency of arm and leg swing during walking,” Biol Cybern, doi 10.1007/s00422-004-0503-5](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00422-004-0503-5) (walking).

5. **During running in place, devices validated for translational running can measure cadence correctly:** the lumbar smart patch had bias <1 step/min at slow/medium intensities against a force plate + MEMS accelerometers (the Garmin Pod failed at fast pace). Source: [Sensors 22(13):4897, doi 10.3390/s22134897](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/22/13/4897).

### Inferences

- Literature validates the torso as a **sensor location** for cadence, using accelerometers at 100+ Hz. Our signal is *vertical position* of hips/shoulders at about 30 samples/s, which is strictly cleaner than acceleration (position is a double integral, so high-frequency noise is lower, not higher). The accuracy benchmark (about ±1 spm with multi-second windows) shows that the torso signal has ample cadence information; the remaining questions are sampling rate and BlazePose jitter (§3).
- **Do not use shoulder rotation, lateral sway, or arm swing as the primary cadence estimator:** these channels tend toward stride frequency (half cadence) — item 4. There is a real risk that the detector would read 80–90 spm when the player runs at 160–180. These can corroborate, but should never be the primary channel.
- The vertical signal during running in place avoids the horizontal/rotational contamination of translational running — and item 5 shows that the signal physics in place is measurable and stable enough for simple lumbar devices.

### Gaps

- No IMU study of running in place reports cadence error broken down by method (smart-patch studies report aggregate bias). Method validations in items 1–3 are almost all for walking/translational running.
- I found no validation of cadence estimation from **vision-measured torso vertical position** (vision literature validates VO/height, not cadence). The vision-to-IMU bridge here is my inference, although the underlying physical signal is the same (torso vertical displacement).

## 3. Signal processing for cadence at ~30 samples/s with jitter

### Conclusion

At about 30 fps and cadence of 2.5–3.2 Hz, there are 9–12 samples per cycle — the sampling rate itself does not limit this signal. The limits are (a) temporal quantization of peak events and (b) correlated jitter from the internal filter. Convergent literature recommends peak detection with hysteresis/time-domain rules for low latency, autocorrelation for noise robustness, and windows of at least 3.5–4 s for autocorrelation or FFT; FFT on short windows has too coarse a frequency resolution to compete with time-domain period measurement. Multiscale peak detection (AMPD) exists specifically for noisy quasi-periodic signals.

### Cited findings

1. **Short windows cost accuracy: at least 4 s is recommended for autocorrelation and power spectrum (PSD)** — 20 subjects on a treadmill at 1–6 km/h, with 2/4/8 s windows: accuracy improves with window length (especially at slow speeds); spectral averaging helps for short windows. Source: [“Accuracy of Estimates of Step Frequency From a Wearable Gait Monitor,” J Mobile Technology in Medicine 2015](https://www.journalmtm.com/2015/accuracy-of-estimates-of-step-frequency-from-a-wearable-gait-monitor/).
2. **Real-time spectral method: 4 s window, about 2 s latency** — RRACE, robust to smartphone position/orientation and irregular sampling. Source: [RRACE, UBC 2014](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/spin/sites/default/files/local_pdfs/2014_RRACERobustRealtimeAlgorithmforCadenceEstimation.pdf).
3. **Embedded autocorrelation: 3.5 s window ≈ 6 steps at 105 spm** — explicit trade-off: longer windows improve accuracy but cost latency and memory. Source: [Sensors 11(8):7314](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/11/8/7314).
4. **AMPD (automatic multiscale-based peak detection)** — a parameter-free method for peaks in noisy periodic/quasi-periodic signals, robust to high- and low-frequency noise; used as a reference detector in quasi-periodic physiological signal (PPG) toolboxes. Sources: [Scholkmann et al. 2012, Algorithms 5(4):588–603, doi 10.3390/a5040588](https://www.mdpi.com/1999-4893/5/4/588); [ppg-beats toolbox](https://ppg-beats.readthedocs.io/en/latest/toolbox/ppg_beat_detectors/).
5. **Time-domain event cadence is more accurate than frequency-domain estimates on short windows:** Run4Vit’s time-domain method reached ±0.69 spm versus ±1.33 for FFT over the same 6 s window — [EMBC 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/embc48229.2022.9871713).
6. Repository context: effective pose rate degrades to about 10–15 poses/s on weak hardware and 5–10 in the worst case (software GL) — `docs/pesquisa-pipeline-cv.md` §7.5 and `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md` item 1.

### Inferences (calculations and engineering reasoning — not cited)

- **Samples per cycle:** at 30 fps and cadence 150–190 spm (2.5–3.17 Hz), there are 9.5–12 samples/cycle — ample for hysteresis-based peak detection. At a degraded 15 poses/s: 4.7–6 samples/cycle, still viable. At 7 poses/s: about 2–3 samples/cycle — marginal; aliasing itself is not the issue (Nyquist at 3.5 Hz exceeds 3.2 Hz), but sampled phase becomes luck-dependent and jitter becomes a large fraction of amplitude. A torso signal **increases tolerance** compared with a knee signal: knee lift is a narrow pulse (~40–50% of the cycle), while vertical oscillation is quasi-sinusoidal — the whole cycle carries phase information, not just its peak.
- **Event quantization at 30 fps:** step period 285–400 ms = 8.5–12 frames. Measuring period from crossings with ±1-frame resolution gives a period error of ±33/333 ≈ ±10% per isolated step — ±15–19 spm. Standard mitigations: (a) interpolate the peak/crossing position at sub-frame resolution (parabolic or linear interpolation between samples — common DSP practice); (b) measure periods over 2–4 steps (median/mean, as `cadenceTracker` already does with median-3 — error falls by about 1/N); (c) use hysteresis at crossing thresholds to reject jitter (already in the current design). With (a)+(b), expected effective error drops to a few spm — compatible with the game’s speed mapping. **I found no primary citation for sub-frame interpolation in this context; it is standard practice, but should be validated in the benchmark.**
- **FFT is weak for this use:** frequency resolution Δf = 1/T — a 4 s window gives 0.25 Hz bins = 15 spm; even with spectral-peak interpolation, literature (item 5) shows time-domain methods are more accurate. FFT/autocorrelation are better used as a *rhythm-presence detector* (is there periodicity at 2–4 Hz?) and a sanity check for the time-domain estimator, not as the primary measurement.
- **Recommended architecture (to test):** keep the current time-domain approach — events using a Schmitt trigger on the vertical hip/shoulder signal — with two changes: (i) the signal becomes continuous and quasi-sinusoidal, so crossings occur twice per cycle (rising and falling); extract one event per cycle at a consistent phase (e.g. an upward crossing of the moving mean); (ii) validate rhythm with autocorrelation in a rolling ~1.5–2 s window as a quality gate, not as the measurement. Perceived start/stop latency would be around one period (~330–400 ms), better than the current LLCM-style method (see `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md` item 4 and the GUD WIP reference cited there, which updates the estimate in fractions of a step).
- Correlated jitter (internal EMA + One Euro, evidence 2–3 in `docs/autocalibracao-tolerancia-perda.md`) produces slow *wander*, not white noise: fast spurious jitter peaks are rare, but the real signal phase is delayed — expect several dozen milliseconds of additional group delay over body sway. Measure using a synthetic sine-wave fixture and real recordings.

### Gaps

- No primary source addresses cadence estimation at **30 samples/s or less** — IMU literature samples at ≥50–100 Hz. The extrapolation is mine.
- I found no direct published comparison of Schmitt trigger/hysteresis with AMPD/autocorrelation for this specific case.

## 4. Jump detection without a fixed baseline — and confusion between jumping and running bounce

### Conclusion

IMU literature detects jumps **without any calibrated baseline**: what identifies a jump is the *shape* of the signal — countermovement (downward motion) → propulsion (rapid rise) → flight → landing, with takeoff marked by peak upward velocity. Applied to position/position derivative, a jump can be separated from running bounce by **amplitude** (hip rise in even a modest jump is 2–5× greater than a half-cycle of bounce) and **temporal signature** (a prior dip + one rapid ascent versus continuous, steady oscillation). Neutral-height calibration can be adaptive (quiet window + slow EMA — already researched in `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` §1); visible feet/legs are not required. Jump/run confusion is the main remaining risk and needs in-app measurement (see verdict, §9).

### Cited findings

1. **Jump takeoff/timing are identified from acceleration/velocity shape, not absolute position:** flight epoch = filtered vertical acceleration <0.5 g for ≥0.15 s; takeoff time is the point of **maximum velocity** within the jump window; 0.2–0.9 s of flight corresponds to jump heights of **4.9–99.3 cm** (`h = g·t²/8`). Source: [accepted manuscript deposited in the University of Jyväskylä repository (JYU), European Journal of Sport Science](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstreams/e59acec7-067a-4553-a991-8e1b67d34ba9/download). CMJ phases using sacral IMU: start of eccentric phase = first downward zero-crossing of free vertical acceleration; eccentric-to-concentric transition = upward zero-crossing of velocity after the negative peak — [Sports Engineering 2025, doi 10.1007/s12283-025-00526-3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12283-025-00526-3). CMJ phases: quiet standing → countermovement (unweighting, braking, propulsion) → flight → landing — [Fathian, University of Alberta, review of wearable methods](https://ualberta.scholaris.ca/server/api/core/bitstreams/bd312ccb-6533-4dbe-841a-65afe5c75727/content).
2. **Jump height from standing to apex includes extension/plantar flexion — the CoM is already elevated at takeoff:** flight-time methods measure only takeoff→apex and underestimate the total; “the CoM is already elevated before the take-off, mainly due to the plantar flexion of the ankle.” Sources: [systematic review PMC10115716](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10115716/); [T&F 2022 narrative review, doi 10.1080/1091367X.2022.2163398](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1091367X.2022.2163398). Direct implication for us: **hip/shoulder rise above the standing baseline is greater than the ballistic “jump height”** — the position signal is stronger than the ballistic minimum.
3. **Countermovement depth in maximal jumps is on the order of 20–30+ cm** (preferred depth was varied over an interval of nearly 30 cm; differences from optimal were 5.1–11.2 cm). Source: [PMC5260520](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5260520/). With shallow jumps (15% less depth), height falls only about 8% — even “small” intentional jumps include a clear dip.
4. **Typical maximal jump height is about 33 cm** (maximal squat jump, 134 female university students: Kinect 33.1 ± 4.3 cm versus video 33.3 ± 4.1 cm, r = 0.909). Source: [AIREX/JAXA repository](https://repository.exst.jaxa.jp/dspace/handle/a-is/899750). Submaximal/small jumps of 10 cm are the lower end of the range studied in flight-time error literature — [PeerJ 17704](https://peerj.com/articles/17704/).
5. **Camera-based jump-height measurement works best at the pelvis and thoracic spine:** a study of 44 subjects with Azure Kinect found mean error −15.8 ± 23.3 mm (pelvis) and +24.2 ± 35.1 mm (thoracic spine); these were the best fiducials among 7 joints — direct evidence that **the torso is enough to quantify a jump**. Source: [JumpMetric, ACM MUM 2024, doi 10.1145/3701571.3701607](https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3701571.3701607).
6. Adaptive quiet-standing baseline: 1 s window, onset threshold at 5 SD from baseline, and a range-based variant (window max–min) — already researched and cited in `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` §1 (Owen 2014, Adlou 2026, I-DT). Not repeated here.

### Inferences

- **Amplitude separability:** translational running bounce has half-amplitude above the mean of about 3–6.5 cm at the pelvis (half of the 6.5–13.1 cm peak-to-peak in item 2 of §1). Even a modest exergame jump has hip rise above standing of about ballistic height (5–20+ cm) **plus** ankle/leg extension at takeoff (item 2). In torso units (~0.5 m), bounce is about 0.13–0.26 torso peak-to-peak, or ~0.07–0.13 on each side of the mean; a small jump is ~0.15–0.3 torso above baseline. There is separation in typical cases, but the tails overlap: a highly bouncy runner (~13 cm peak-to-peak) versus a minimal hop (~8–10 cm of hip rise). So amplitude must be combined with velocity and/or a dip signature, and measured in-app.
- **Shape/frequency separability:** running is steady and quasi-sinusoidal at 2.5–3.2 Hz; a jump is an isolated event with a preceding negative dip and an ascent faster than any running half-cycle (takeoff is explosive propulsion — peak upward velocity, item 1). A detector that requires (dip or recent neutral position) → high-velocity ascent → return is directly supported by items 1–3.
- **Velocity calculations (my own, using a sinusoidal approximation):** maximum velocity of a sinusoidal oscillation is `v = π·f·A_p-p`. With `A_p-p = 0.11–0.29 torso` (§1) and `f = 2.5–3.2 Hz`, running bounce reaches vertical peak velocities around **0.9–2.9 torso/s** (typical center ~1.5 torso/s; amplitude and frequency are inversely related, compressing the extremes). Jump takeoff velocity is `v = √(2·g·h)`: h = 5 cm ⇒ **1.0 m/s ≈ 2 torso/s**; 10 cm ⇒ 1.4 m/s ≈ 2.8 torso/s; 20 cm ⇒ 2.0 m/s ≈ 4 torso/s (torso ≈ 0.5 m). Interpretation: (a) running bounce **comfortably exceeds** the current `jumpDetector` velocity threshold (0.5 torso/s) — consistent with the confusion zone in `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md` item 2 and a warning that the same conflict will exist with a torso signal unless thresholds are retuned; (b) an upward-velocity gate around ~1.5–2 torso/s separates most bounce from any real jump ≥5 cm, but the tails overlap (a ~5 cm hop versus very bouncy running) — velocity needs amplitude and/or evidence of a dip. These are theoretical estimates under a sinusoidal approximation; measure with real recordings before fixing values.
- **Adaptive baseline is viable but secondary:** IMU design shows detection can work with no baseline at all (using shape). In practice, a standing hip baseline is still useful for amplitude and can be estimated continuously during quiet standing detected with a dispersion window (I-DT, already covered in the research notes), without a leg-based calibration phase.
- Jumping **while running** (the Hurdle Rush case): a jump during running in place interrupts the rhythmic pattern — typically the player “loads” with a deeper step and flight lasts longer than running-bounce flight. A promising discriminator is duration above/below the mean (a running cycle never stays in the upper half for more than about half a period, ~150–200 ms). **This is a design hypothesis, not a cited finding — validate with recordings.**

### Gaps

- No published figures for hip amplitude/velocity in **small in-place exergame jumps** (the literature mostly covers maximal athletic jumps or test CMJs). Small heights (5–15 cm) are documented only as the tail of test distributions.
- No published validation of real-time monocular RGB vision jump detection (validation literature measures jump *height* offline using Kinect/depth or motion capture — `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` already records this gap).
- Actual running-in-place bounce amplitude (see §1) is the unknown that most affects the jump/run margin.

## 5. BlazePose/MediaPipe with torso-only framing — what is guaranteed

### Conclusion

Upper-body-only is an **explicitly designed and supported** use case: the training set includes cases where only hips + shoulders can be annotated, training uses heavy occlusion augmentation, and the MediaPipe team says that since 2021 the full-body model “handles those cases well.” The initial detector **requires the face** — this is the documented minimum visible-body requirement. What is NOT guaranteed: (a) positions of invisible points (they are hallucinated best guesses, §6); (b) any controlled accuracy figure for visible points when the lower body is outside the frame; or (c) reliable visibility/presence scores in every configuration.

### Cited findings

1. **The person detector is an adapted face detector (proxy), assuming a visible head.** BlazePose §2.2: “we use a fast on-device face detector as a proxy for a person detector. This face detector predicts additional person-specific alignment parameters: the middle point between the person's hips, the size of the circle circumscribing the whole person, and the incline angle of the line connecting the shoulder and hip midpoints” and “we make the strong, yet for AR applications valid, assumption that the head of the person should always be visible for our single-person use case.” Sources: [arXiv 2006.10204 §2.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10204); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/on-device-real-time-body-pose-tracking-with-mediapipe-blazepose/). ML Kit says similarly: “**The user's face must be present in order to detect a pose.** Pose detection works best when the subject's entire body is visible in the frame, but it also detects a partial body pose. In that case the landmarks that are not recognized are assigned coordinates outside of the image.” — [developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/pose-detection](https://developers.google.com/ml-kit/vision/pose-detection). The model card lists “a head that is not visible” as out of scope — [pose_model_card.pdf](https://developers.google.com/static/ml-kit/images/vision/pose-detection/pose_model_card.pdf) (also cited in `docs/pesquisa-inclinacao-tronco.md`).
2. **The tracker’s training dataset is deliberately restricted to whole bodies being visible OR hips + shoulders being confidently annotatable:** “We restrict our dataset to those cases where either the whole person is visible, or where hips and shoulders keypoints can be confidently annotated. To ensure the model supports heavy occlusions that are not present in the dataset, we use substantial occlusion-simulating augmentation.” Source: [arXiv 2006.10204 §2.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.10204). In other words, **visible shoulders + hips are exactly the minimum training-distribution configuration** — upper-body calibration (shoulders + hips + face) is within the designed envelope.
3. **MediaPipe team: the full-body model was improved to handle an invisible lower body, and the dedicated upper-body model was retired:** “We previously included an upper-body-only model because the full-body model didn't handle well the cases where the lower body is not visible. However, we've improved the full-body model (across all model complexities: lite, full, heavy) such that it now handles those cases well and a special upper-body-only model is no longer needed. [...] if desired you could simply ignore the pose landmarks that correspond to the lower body (i.e., index 25~32).” Source: [issue #1995, comment by MediaPipe team member chuoling, 2021](https://github.com/google/mediapipe/issues/1995), referencing [release v0.8.4](https://github.com/google/mediapipe/releases/tag/v0.8.4).
4. **The tracking loop uses a crop aligned and scaled to the estimated whole body** — the ROI is centered at the hip midpoint, rotated according to the shoulder–hip line, and scaled to the square bounding the entire body (Vitruvian man), not only the visible part — [arXiv 2006.10204 §2.6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.10204) (“The scale is estimated so that all the body points fit in a square bounding box circumscribed around the body”). The detector runs on the first frame and whenever the tracker loses presence — [research blog](https://research.google/blog/on-device-real-time-body-pose-tracking-with-mediapipe-blazepose/); `docs/pesquisa-pipeline-cv.md` §6.
5. **Minimum model visibility requirement:** as §2.6 of the paper states, the design explicitly targets “upper body-only or when the majority of person body is out of scene” (already established in earlier repository research and repeated here for completeness).

### Inferences

- With torso framing (face + shoulders + hips), initial detection works because the face is present (item 1) and the configuration is in the training distribution (item 2). The tracker ROI extends **beyond the bottom edge of the frame** (the circle bounds the estimated whole body, including predicted legs) — the crop includes an off-image/padded area; leg keypoints are emitted in that space as extrapolations (§6). This does not break the pipeline — it is exactly the scenario in Figure 5 of the paper — but it means leg landmarks carry no actual visual evidence in this setup.
- Subtle risk: because the ROI is scaled to the *estimated whole body*, the fraction of the crop devoted to the visible torso is smaller than in a torso-only crop; effective shoulder/hip resolution is lower than it would be with a torso-only model. I found no measurement of this effect; the team’s statement (item 3) is that this case is handled well. Record this as a theoretical, unmeasured trade-off.
- Framing that cuts off the face (e.g. a low camera aimed at the chest) **breaks initial detection** — a hard requirement (item 1). Onboarding must ensure face + shoulders + hips, not just torso.

### Gaps

- **No controlled published accuracy measurement for visible keypoints (shoulders/hips) comparing full-body and upper-body-only framing.** The model card reports PDJ/PCK@0.2 aggregated by geography, without breakdown by framing — [pose_model_card.pdf](https://developers.google.com/static/ml-kit/images/vision/pose-detection/pose_model_card.pdf).
- I found no official statement on crop/ROI behavior when the estimated circle extends outside the frame (padding, etc.) — this is inferred from §2.6.

## 6. Hallucinated positions for out-of-frame legs — form and risks

### Conclusion

The model **always emits 33 points** and predicts a plausible **average/default pose** for invisible points — it does not collapse them into the hips or emit random noise. Hallucinated legs look like a plausible standing continuation below the hips, often outside the frame (`y > 1`). Scores do not always reveal this: documented cases have visibility/presence around 0.9–0.99 for invisible points, although on average visibility <0.5 predicts large error and `presence` is the semantically appropriate signal for “outside the frame.” Treating hallucinated knees/ankles as real is invalidated by the sources — cadence/leg gates must filter by presence, not visibility alone.

### Cited findings

1. **Official model card:** trained and evaluated on visible and occluded points; an occluded point is annotated as a “best guess” with a default pose; the model “degrades gracefully by predicting average point location” (also qualitatively evaluated on users with missing limbs/prostheses). Source: [BlazePose GHUM 3D model card, Bias section](https://developers.google.com/static/ml-kit/images/vision/pose-detection/pose_model_card.pdf).
2. **User evidence (Holistic, upper-body-only):** all 33 points are always emitted; invisible points appear “in vaguely plausible places, however undeniably inaccurate” — checked by plotting their 3D coordinates. Source: [issue #5315](https://github.com/google/mediapipe/issues/5315).
3. **Coordinates outside [0,1] for off-scene points confirmed in practice:** an accepted StackOverflow answer notes y > 1 when the estimated point is below the field of view; documentation says x/y are normalized to the frame, but points are still estimated outside it. Source: [StackOverflow 76627289](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/76627289/mediapipe-pose-landmarks-out-of-range-0-1); consistent with ML Kit documentation (item 1 in §5). A documented case had hips at **y ≈ 0.996–1.009** (at/beyond the bottom edge) with **visibility 0.87–0.94** but **presence 0.53–0.65** — visibility can remain high when a point is effectively out of frame; presence carries more information about out-of-frame status. Source: [issue #4462](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/4462).
4. **Scores are not reliable in every configuration:** with a person facing away, nose/eyes/front-facing joints had visibility/presence around 0.99 in every model (lite/full/heavy) — [issue #5197](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/5197). Occluded hands in multi-person scenes still produce hallucinated landmarks — [issue #5806](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/5806). An occluded rear leg in side view shows persistent instability, left/right swaps, and foot misplacement even in version 1.2.3 (official maintainer response, 2026) — [issue #4969](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/issues/4969).
5. **Quantitative counterpoint — scores carry information on average:** joint-position errors (multi-camera MediaPipe motion capture × Vicon) “increased markedly below a visibility of 0.5,” and variance also rises. Source: [Chen et al., PMC13766164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13766164/).

### Inferences

- **Expected shape of hallucinated legs when the torso is framed:** a plausible “standing” continuation below the hips (average pose — item 1), extending beyond the bottom edge with y > 1 (items 2–3). Not collapsed into the hips; not white noise.
- **Hallucinated knees will move vertically with the hips:** the hallucinated pose is articulated from visible points; when the hips rise/fall during running bounce, the entire phantom leg rises/falls rigidly with them. For a knee-lift detector **relative to the hip** (knee−hip, as in our `cadenceTracker`), the phantom signal is nearly static (hallucinated leg geometry does not change because there is no visual evidence of flexion), plus estimation noise — so the net effect is **loss of cadence**, not much false cadence. A naive detector of absolute knee height, however, would measure hip oscillation *as if it were* knee lift — a structural false positive. Two lessons: (a) never consume a leg without a presence gate; (b) if legs are ever used as corroboration, the proper gate is high presence + temporal consistency, not visibility alone (items 3–4).
- Given item 4 (documented score failures) and item 5 (error rises below visibility 0.5), the safe policy is **high presence AND visibility + geometric sanity checks** (points inside [−m, 1+m] with margin) before using any leg landmark. The repository’s current 0.55 threshold on min(vis,pres) points in the right direction; the evidence suggests giving presence special weight for out-of-frame cases.

### Gaps

- No published quantitative measure of typical hallucinated knee/ankle error or position as a function of the fraction of the body outside the frame (only qualitative evidence and issues).
- I found no official documentation on how the model chooses between “standing, legs extended” and other default poses for invisible points — only the model-card statement in item 1.

## 7. Stability of torso vertical position in image space — confounders (brief notes)

This topic is mostly derivable and kept short as instructed.

### Cited findings

1. x/y are normalized by image width/height; z has its origin at the hip midpoint — [Pose Landmarker guide](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/pose_landmarker/ios). World landmarks are hip-centered and camera-aligned, **not gravity-aligned**; camera pitch mixes vertical with depth, and roll enters one-to-one as tilt — `docs/pesquisa-inclinacao-tronco.md` §1 (findings already cited there to primary sources).
2. Existing calibration already normalizes y by image `torsoHeight` and stores `hipY0` — `src/cv/protocol.ts` (via `docs/analise-gargalos-corrida.md`).

### Inferences

- **Camera distance:** normalizing hip y by torsoHeight in the same frame cancels scale to first order (similar triangles — apparent size and displacement scale together). Second-order residuals arise from nonlinear perspective at short distances and changes in average geometry as the player moves toward/away from camera. Torso normalization does not correct a distance change in the absolute `hipY0` reference — already documented as drift in `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md`.
- **Camera tilt/roll:** vertical tilt (low camera looking upward) compresses/apparently tilts vertical scale — real vertical movement remains vertical in the image if the camera only pitches (the projection remains on the image y-axis to first order, with cos(tilt) compression); roll contaminates lean, not vertical oscillation. Small effect for cadence (oscillatory signal — periodic component matters, not absolute level) and relevant to jump only through scale.
- **Sitting versus standing:** the hip drops ~15–25 cm (order of magnitude — no primary source; derivable from anthropometry), indistinguishable from a “deep squat” to a baseline-dependent detector. Since the proposed detector depends on shape/amplitude, not absolute level, the risk is an adaptive baseline anchored while sitting — mitigate by requiring detected quiet standing + torsoHeight consistency with the calibrated value.
- **Stationary wave:** cadence signal is AC — any constant offset (tilt, image position, camera height) is irrelevant to measuring *cadence*; it matters only for jumping (amplitude relative to baseline) and lane/posture. This supports treating cadence as a frequency/shape problem rather than a level problem.

## 8. Precedents — webcam-based running/jump detection without relying on feet

### Cited findings (projects/documentation — primary sources for this question by their nature)

1. **jumpcount (aminnj)** — self-contained web page, in-browser MediaPipe, counts jump rope “via **peak detection of the mean torso vertical position**”; user instruction: “make sure the four corners around your **torso** are visible and begin jumping” — jump detection from torso alone, in production since 2021. Source: [github.com/aminnj/jumpcount](https://github.com/aminnj/jumpcount).
2. **pose-runner (cheegrace)** — endless runner entirely in the browser (Canvas 2D, no build); instruction: “stand back so your **shoulders** are in frame”; controls: torso lean to steer (proportional) and raised hand to jump — design built entirely around torso/shoulder framing. Source: [github.com/cheegrace/pose-runner](https://github.com/cheegrace/pose-runner/blob/main/README.md).
3. **Polaris (qiuethan)** — web multiplayer fitness platformer (1st place, Hack the 6ix 2025): MediaPipe + “sliding window gesture recognition” with confidence scoring for **run, jump, crouch**, 2 players on one webcam, target latency below 300 ms. Sources: [github.com/qiuethan/Polaris](https://github.com/qiuethan/Polaris); [Devpost](https://devpost.com/software/polaris-vlp1wm). (Little public technical detail; I could not find the running algorithm itself.)
4. **ai-sport-desktop (Gakiwoo)** — desktop app (Tauri + React) with MediaPipe: **vertical jump in place detected from a hip-y peak + flight-time verification**; jump rope from wrist rotation + hip/ankle displacement; state machine with hysteresis. Source: [github.com/Gakiwoo/ai-sport-desktop](https://github.com/Gakiwoo/ai-sport-desktop) (README in Chinese — algorithm table indexed).
5. **Counterexamples that require feet** — useful contrast: the Pace prototype detects running in place “from **foot** motion” ([github.com/DhanvinG/Pace](https://github.com/DhanvinG/Pace)); a jump-rope-counting tutorial requires ankle visibility >0.9 on both feet ([CSDN](https://blog.csdn.net/qq_21201267/article/details/133564518)). In other words, the popular “naive” approach relies on feet — and breaks exactly in our scenario.
6. **Official MediaPipe pose-classification/repetition-counting guide:** EMA for noise + **double-threshold hysteresis** in the rep state machine — already cited in `docs/autocalibracao-tolerancia-perda.md` evidence 12, with pinned link: [pose_classification.md](https://github.com/google-ai-edge/mediapipe/blob/3eb8983cde1517fdff4abbfb33586316af6d45be/docs/solutions/pose_classification.md).
7. **JumpMetric** (see §4 item 5) — pelvis/thoracic spine as the best jump-height fiducials with a depth camera: the closest academic validation for our use case.

### Inferences

- The most relevant precedent (jumpcount) uses exactly the architecture proposed here: signal = mean torso vertical position; detector = peaks. This is engineering evidence (not controlled research) that torso-signal SNR for jumping/rope skipping is sufficient under real webcam conditions.
- No precedent found for **running-in-place cadence from vertical torso oscillation** — the design space is open. Existing examples either measure running from feet (not viable here) or use sliding-window gesture ML (black box, with no published method). This is a precedent gap, not a physics gap.

## 9. Verdict

**(a) Can running-in-place cadence be detected from the torso? YES — high confidence in the physics and IMU literature, pending measurement of amplitude in our pipeline.**

- Recommended signal: image-space y of the hip midpoint (landmarks 23/24) — possibly averaged with shoulders (11/12) — normalized by frame torsoHeight, or world y with the caveats in `docs/pesquisa-inclinacao-tronco.md` (not gravity-aligned; real vertical oscillation appears mixed if the camera is pitched — prefer normalized image coordinates).
- Expected signal quality: quasi-sinusoidal oscillation at the **exact step frequency** (2 oscillations/stride — evidence in §1 item 1), expected pelvic amplitude on the order of 3–6.5 cm on either side of the mean (6.5–13.1 cm peak-to-peak in translational running — §1 item 2; torso ≥ CoM — §1 item 4), i.e. ~0.06–0.13 torso-height per side. Typical frequency is 2.5–3.2 Hz at common cadences (150–190 spm; running in place measured at 172–178 spm — §1 item 6). The key unknown is amplitude **in place** (never measured in literature — §1 gap) and attenuation by the internal One Euro filter at 3 Hz.
- Method recommended by literature: time-domain events with hysteresis + median of periods (IMU benchmark ±0.7–1.3 spm with 6 s windows — §2 item 1), with short autocorrelation as a rhythm gate; FFT only as corroboration (coarse resolution — §3). Theoretical start/stop latency is about one period (~330–400 ms).

**(b) Can jumping be detected without leg calibration? YES — by signal shape, not absolute level.**

- A jump is identified by: dip/neutral → rapid rise (peak upward velocity) → hip amplitude well above the running-bounce envelope → return. An adaptive baseline from quiet standing + a dispersion window (already researched) improves robustness but is not strictly necessary — IMU literature detects jumps from signal shape (§4 item 1).
- Bounce/jump separation: good in the typical range (jump 2–5× higher than a bounce half-cycle; takeoff for a ≥5 cm jump gives ~2+ torso/s versus bounce’s sinusoidal peak ~0.9–2.9 torso/s — there is some margin, but it is not large), marginal in the tails (high-bounce running × small hop). The current velocity threshold (0.5 torso/s) is **below** expected bounce peaks — retune to ~1.5–2 torso/s using real data (§4 calculations). Combine amplitude + velocity + evidence of a dip; never use amplitude alone.

**(c) What MediaPipe guarantees / does not guarantee for upper-body-only framing:**

- Guaranteed by design/documentation: visible face + shoulders + hips is within the training distribution (§5 item 2); upper-body-only is an explicitly designed case (§2.6 of the paper); the team says the full-body model handles an out-of-frame lower body well since 2021 (§5 item 3); output is always complete (33 points), with coordinates outside [0,1] for off-scene points and per-point scores (§5 item 1, §6 item 3).
- NOT guaranteed: positions of invisible points — they are hallucinated average/default poses (model card, §6 item 1) and carry no visual evidence; scores fail in some documented configurations (visibility/presence ~0.99 on invisible points — §6 item 4), although visibility <0.5 predicts large error on average (§6 item 5); no controlled accuracy figure for visible keypoints under partial framing (§5 gap). Initial detection requires the **face** (§5 item 1) — framing must include the head.
- Direct consequence: calibration can be torso-only (face + shoulders + hips, the model’s minimum training condition), and **every use of a leg landmark must be gated by presence + visibility + geometric sanity** — or the legs should simply not be used.

**(d) What remains uncertain and must be measured in-app:**

1. Real peak-to-peak vertical oscillation amplitude of hips/shoulders while running in place at 140–200 spm, measured by Pose Landmarker Lite *after* its internal One Euro filter, at actual game distances/framing — the number that determines cadence/jump thresholds. (§1 gap.)
2. Attenuation/group delay of the filter chain (internal EMA + internal 1€ + app 1€) for a 2.5–3.2 Hz sine with amplitude 3–8 cm in normalized units — measure with a synthetic fixture and a recording. (§3.)
3. Resting jitter of normalized hip/shoulder y (p95 of 200 ms windows) — needed to set the jump neutral band and Schmitt hysteresis (§3 in `docs/pesquisa-robustez-gestos.md` already gives the recipe).
4. Behavior of hallucinated legs in our exact framing (default position, emitted presence/visibility scores) — to validate the gate. (§6.)
5. Empirical jump-versus-bounce separation: record running-in-place sessions with occasional jumps and measure maximum hip-amplitude distributions per cycle in both modes — actual overlap determines whether amplitude + velocity is enough or whether the rhythm must be modeled (subtract periodic component). (§4.)
6. Verify the effect of full-body crop resolution on shoulder/hip accuracy under partial versus full-body framing (trade-off in §5; no source measures it).
7. **Torso compression × per-frame normalization** (raised during implementation review): synthetic fixtures translate shoulders + hips rigidly, but in real running torso height *shrinks* at step frequency (hip/knee flexion during stance). Since the channel normalizes by the current frame’s torso height, ±5% compression injects a false rise term of about ∓0.07 torso at the same step frequency — it does not break cadence counting (same frequency, fixed phase), but contaminates amplitude-dependent readings (jump gates, oscillation floor). Measure actual compression in recordings; if large, consider normalizing by smoothed rather than instantaneous torso height.
8. **False-trigger boundary during amplitude transients** (also raised during review): jump/bounce separation by phase (high velocity coinciding with high rise) holds exactly only in steady sinusoidal conditions. During a ramp-up in bounce amplitude, the amplitude threshold is crossed midway through the rise, while velocity is still high — the combined gates can pass. The estimated boundary is a half-amplitude input around 0.12–0.13 torso (24–26 cm peak-to-peak), above the literature range but never measured for running in place. Characterize using ramps in real recordings.
9. **Colored noise and post-stop sway** (also raised during review): noise fixtures use clean sine waves at frame-index frequencies; real estimator jitter is colored/correlated, and post-exercise sway (heavy breathing, weight shift) has a low-frequency component that passes through the filter unattenuated. The implemented structural immunity (hysteresis band with floor 0.012 torso ⇒ bounce below ~11 mm peak-to-peak never produces a step) must be validated against measured resting jitter from item 3 — if actual jitter exceeds ~5 mm half-amplitude, the floor and the amplitude-gate floor must both rise.
